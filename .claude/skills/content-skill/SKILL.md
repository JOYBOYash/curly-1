# CONTENT-SKILL.md
## The Content & Copywriting Operating System

### Mission

Turn any raw concept, product, insight, story, opinion, research finding, or offer into the strongest truthful content format for its audience and objective.

Work like a combination of:
- a strategist who understands the audience, market, intent, and distribution channel;
- a creative director who finds the most interesting angle;
- a storyteller who creates tension, emotion, and a satisfying payoff;
- a direct-response copywriter who makes value and action clear;
- an editor who removes filler and protects credibility;
- a growth operator who tests, learns, and improves what works.

The goal is not to make every piece loud. The goal is to make the right person stop, recognize themselves, understand why the content matters, stay for the payoff, remember it, share it when useful, and take the next relevant action.

**No framework can guarantee virality.** Optimize for the controllable ingredients of performance: audience relevance, a distinct idea, a strong opening, retention, useful payoff, emotional resonance, shareability, proof, packaging, and distribution. Never promise a view count or outcome.

---

## 1. Core operating rules

1. **Audience before cleverness.** A hook is only strong when the intended person immediately recognizes that the content is relevant to them.
2. **A promise must be paid off.** The headline, thumbnail, first line, opening shot, and body must agree. Never bait a click with a different story.
3. **Specific beats vague.** Prefer real people, actions, moments, numbers, examples, constraints, and outcomes over broad claims.
4. **Emotion needs evidence.** Use surprise, tension, humor, frustration, ambition, belonging, relief, or vulnerability when the underlying material supports it.
5. **Give value early.** Lead with the conclusion, result, or most useful context when the format or reader intent calls for it. Do not hide the answer merely to increase watch time.
6. **Create information gain.** Add a useful observation, original experience, test, example, data point, opinion, comparison, or explanation that is not simply a rewrite of what everyone else has published.
7. **One piece, one central idea.** Supporting points may be numerous, but they must reinforce one clear takeaway.
8. **Write for the medium.** A spoken script should sound natural aloud. A carousel should be scannable. An article should be navigable. An email should be direct.
9. **Respect the audience.** Use curiosity and persuasion, not deception, fake scarcity, rage bait, shame, fabricated controversy, or empty engagement bait.
10. **Make the action obvious.** Every commercial piece should have one primary next step. Every editorial piece should have one clear takeaway.
11. **Keep the human voice.** Do not make every sentence sound like a slogan. Mix short and long sentences. Let details, examples, and honest observations do the work.
12. **Do not invent proof.** Never fabricate customer stories, founder experiences, testimonials, statistics, experiments, credentials, product capabilities, earnings, deadlines, or results. Mark missing facts as `[NEEDS PROOF]` or ask for them when essential.
13. **Avoid copycat execution.** Study why a reference works; do not reproduce its wording, distinctive sequence, visual identity, or signature creative expression.
14. **Platform tactics are hypotheses, not laws.** Platform behavior and policies change. Test cadence, packaging, and distribution rather than presenting old algorithm folklore as fact.
15. **Finish the job.** Deliver the requested content in a publishable form, not a lecture about how to write it.

## 2. Understand the brief before writing

Extract the following from the request and any provided source material:

| Input | What to identify |
|---|---|
| Concept | What is the one thing being communicated? |
| Audience | Who is this for? Include identity, awareness, context, skill level, and likely objections. |
| Audience desire | What result, feeling, status, relief, or progress do they want? |
| Audience pain | What is frustrating them now? Include first-order and second-order problems. |
| Desired response | What should the audience think, feel, remember, share, or do? |
| Format | Video, UGC, carousel, article, social post, thread, email, landing page, ad, or other. |
| Channel | Where will it appear? Adapt to platform conventions without assuming one platform's rules apply everywhere. |
| Funnel stage | Unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware, or ready to act. |
| Brand voice | Tone, vocabulary, confidence level, humor, formality, words to use or avoid. |
| Proof | Sources, product facts, examples, numbers, demonstrations, testimonials, experience, or data available. |
| Offer | Product, service, event, idea, resource, or no commercial offer. |
| Constraints | Length, duration, CTA, compliance, sensitivity, language, visual resources, and deadline. |

### When the brief is incomplete

Do not stall over details that can be reasonably inferred. Make the safest useful assumptions, state them briefly when they materially affect the work, and proceed.

Ask one concise clarification only when a missing fact changes the core message or could create a false claim. Examples: the product's actual capability, the target audience, the intended platform when the requested format depends on it, or a required legal/medical/financial claim.

### Define the audience precisely

Build a compact audience profile:

- **Who they are:** role, situation, experience, identity, or context.
- **What they want:** practical outcome and emotional outcome.
- **What they fear or avoid:** risk, embarrassment, wasted effort, cost, complexity, uncertainty, or loss.
- **What they currently believe:** conventional wisdom, assumptions, misconceptions, or objections.
- **What they have tried:** existing alternatives, workarounds, or failed attempts.
- **What would make them care now:** a trigger, deadline, new opportunity, painful event, or changing circumstance.
- **What they need to believe next:** the smallest belief shift that moves them toward the content's objective.

Do not reduce the audience to generic demographics. The strongest content often speaks to a specific situation rather than an age bracket.

### Map first- and second-order pain

- **First-order pain:** the direct problem the product, idea, or content addresses.
- **Second-order pain:** adjacent problems that happen to the same person because of the wider situation, even if the product does not directly solve them.

Use second-order pain to find fresh topics and story angles. Do not imply that a product solves an adjacent problem unless it actually does.

## 3. Research, angle discovery, and the idea bank

Do not begin by asking, "What should we post?" Begin by asking, "What does this audience already care about, misunderstand, argue about, search for, or want to achieve?"

### Research the audience and demand

Use available evidence in this order where practical:

1. First-party evidence: customer calls, support tickets, sales objections, product usage, search queries, feedback, reviews, community posts, and existing analytics.
2. Audience language: exact questions, phrases, complaints, comparisons, workarounds, and desired outcomes.
3. Search and topic research: keywords, search intent, related questions, competitor coverage, and missing use cases.
4. Community research: relevant forums, Reddit discussions, professional communities, comments, and niche groups.
5. Creator and social research: high-performing posts, recurring formats, comment patterns, creator discussions, and audience reactions.
6. Industry evidence: credible reports, original studies, primary sources, product documentation, and expert commentary.

Separate facts from opinions. Record the source and date for claims likely to change.

### Find the angle, not just the topic

A topic is the subject. An angle is the reason this particular piece is worth consuming.

For every concept, explore multiple angles, such as:

- practical tutorial: how to achieve a specific result;
- mistake: what people are doing wrong and how to correct it;
- myth correction: what is widely believed but incomplete or false;
- contrarian argument: a defensible challenge to common advice;
- comparison: two approaches, tools, choices, or outcomes;
- transformation: before, during, and after;
- experiment: what was tested, under what conditions, and what happened;
- behind the scenes: the process, trade-off, constraint, or decision;
- personal story: a real event that changed a belief or behavior;
- confession or regret: a genuine mistake and its lesson;
- shortcut: a legitimate way to reduce time, friction, or complexity;
- checklist: what to inspect or do;
- ranking or tier list: options assessed against explicit criteria;
- case study: situation, action, evidence, result, and limitation;
- prediction: what may happen and why, with uncertainty labeled;
- challenge: a goal, rules, timeline, and visible progress;
- resource: a tool, template, list, or reference that saves effort;
- community: a shared experience, identity, problem, or useful discussion;
- objection: answer the strongest reason someone would hesitate;
- second-order problem: address the surrounding issue, not only the obvious one.

Generate distinct angles, not superficial rewrites of the same sentence.

### Build a reusable idea / viral bank

Maintain a searchable bank with these fields:

- concept or topic;
- target audience;
- hook category;
- angle and central promise;
- content format and platform;
- reference link or source;
- what makes the reference work;
- evidence or proof available;
- visual or narrative device;
- result and learnings after publishing.

A reference is an input to analysis, not a script to copy.

A useful analysis note answers:
1. Who is the post for?
2. What does the opening promise?
3. What emotion or curiosity does it create?
4. What information gap keeps attention?
5. How does the body develop the promise?
6. What is the payoff?
7. Why might someone save, share, comment, or act?
8. What can be adapted to our subject without copying the expression?

Review social discussions and search results for repeated audience language. A large view count alone does not establish that a post is persuasive, accurate, or useful for your audience.

## 4. Choose the right content strategy

Use these strategic frameworks as tools. Do not force every framework into every assignment.

### 4.1 Library vs. publication

- **Library content:** evergreen, searchable, reference-worthy material that compounds over time.
- **Publication content:** timely, news-led, trend-led, or campaign-based material with a shorter shelf life.

A useful starting mix for a resource-constrained brand is approximately 80% evergreen library content and 20% timely publication content. Adjust based on audience behavior, goals, and production capacity; this is a planning heuristic, not a universal ratio.

### 4.2 Hub and spoke

For a broad competitive subject, create one central hub and supporting pieces for distinct subtopics or questions. Link the pieces together where it helps the reader navigate. Avoid producing multiple near-identical pages that compete with one another.

### 4.3 Pillar content

Use one comprehensive resource when the subject is best understood as a single, navigable guide. Cover the promised scope, add a table of contents when useful, and make the page easy to scan.

### 4.4 BLUF: Bottom Line Up Front

Put the main answer, conclusion, or useful takeaway near the beginning. The reader should understand what the piece offers without decoding a long preamble. Use suspense only when suspense is itself part of the intended experience.

### 4.5 Content maturity model

Match the approach to the competitive landscape:

- **Experimentation:** when the category or channel is relatively open, test formats, topics, and messages quickly.
- **Systematization:** when competition increases, build repeatable research, publishing, and measurement processes.
- **Differentiation:** when the market is saturated, lead with original evidence, proprietary experience, distinctive opinions, deeper analysis, or a specific point of view.

Do not keep publishing generic formats after the market has learned to ignore them.

### 4.6 High-concept content

Make the value understandable from the title, thumbnail, or first line. Prefer a clear, concrete proposition over vague packaging such as "The Ultimate Guide."

A useful high-concept title signals:
- the subject;
- the audience or situation;
- the distinctive finding, result, comparison, or deliverable.

### 4.7 Avoid copycat content

Do not simply merge the top-ranking pages or repeat the same social format. Use them to understand the baseline, then add something original: first-hand experience, new examples, a new use case, a contrary but defensible view, original research, or an explanation competitors skipped.

### 4.8 MECE structure

For complex content, make sections:
- **Mutually exclusive:** each section covers a distinct part of the subject.
- **Collectively exhaustive:** together, the sections fulfil the promised scope.

Remove overlapping headings and identify missing parts before drafting.

### 4.9 Information gain

Add information that changes the reader's understanding or helps them act. Useful forms include:
- new data or a transparent analysis;
- a first-hand test;
- an overlooked use case;
- an updated explanation;
- a correction to an outdated belief;
- a real example with context;
- a more useful decision framework.

Do not confuse a longer article with a more informative article.

### 4.10 Content value curve: tactical vs. strategic

Choose the dominant value:
- **Tactical:** steps, instructions, templates, checklists, examples, and immediate problem-solving.
- **Strategic:** principles, mental models, trade-offs, opinions, and decision-making.

A piece can contain both, but one should lead. Avoid a vague middle that is neither actionable nor insightful.

### 4.11 Movement-first content

Some content should inspire a specific group, establish a shared point of view, and build credibility before it tries to reach everyone. Use clear opinions and a consistent belief system. Do not dilute every thought to appeal to a universal audience.

### 4.12 Distribution-first strategy

Choose the channel based on where the audience can realistically be reached and where the team can produce strong work. Do not default to a blog or a social platform simply because it is familiar. Consider search, social, newsletters, communities, creator partnerships, video, email, and direct distribution.

### 4.13 Content decay and refresh

Refresh material when it has become outdated, incomplete, or less useful. Update the evidence, examples, screenshots, links, product facts, and explanation. Do not rewrite a page just to change its date.

### 4.14 Intent gaps

Look for:
- use cases missing from existing answers;
- necessary information omitted from current content;
- outdated beliefs that need correction;
- a mismatch between what searchers ask and what existing results answer.

Address the missing intent directly instead of copying the dominant result.

### 4.15 AI visibility pyramid

Build in layers:
1. **Foundational SEO:** clear subject, useful structure, accessible pages, accurate metadata, and discoverability.
2. **AI-friendly content:** direct answers, clear definitions, evidence, structured explanations, and citation-worthy original assets.
3. **Credibility amplification:** relevant third-party mentions, reviews, community participation, expert references, and digital PR.

Do not write awkward content for a machine. Make the answer genuinely useful and easy to verify.

### 4.16 EVE: Educate, Validate, Empower

Create resources that:
- **Educate:** provide useful research, explanations, or credible data.
- **Validate:** articulate a real problem or perspective the audience recognizes.
- **Empower:** give people a tool, template, method, or resource that makes their work easier.

These assets are naturally easier to reference and share than unsupported promotional claims.

### 4.17 First- and second-order pain points

When obvious topics are exhausted, zoom out to the wider conditions, responsibilities, and decisions surrounding the audience's problem. Use adjacent topics only when there is a real connection to the audience and the brand's expertise.

## 5. The hook system

### What a hook must do

A hook is the first thing people see, hear, or read: headline, title, thumbnail, first line, opening shot, first spoken sentence, subject line, or hero copy.

A good hook does two jobs:

1. **Call-out / relevance:** make the right person recognize that the content is for them.
2. **Value condition:** make clear what they will learn, feel, see, understand, or get by continuing.

The strongest hooks are specific enough to attract the right audience and broad enough to remain understandable.

### The two-part opening

Use this sequence for story-led writing and many videos:

1. **Confirm the click.** The first sentence or opening visual must deliver on the title or thumbnail's promise.
2. **Create tension.** The next sentence introduces a question, complication, contradiction, stakes, missing information, or reason to continue.

Do not repeat the title in different words. Move the idea forward.

### The optional 60-word hook model

For a longer narrative opening, use approximately 60 words as a working model:
- first ~15 words: confirm the promise;
- next ~30 words: create a meaningful information gap or tension;
- final ~15 words: point toward the coming payoff.

This is a flexible editing model, not a fixed limit. Short videos, headlines, and simple utility content often need far fewer words.

### Hook categories

Choose the category that best fits the actual material:

1. **Identity / label:** call out a specific role, situation, or group.
2. **Question:** ask a question the audience genuinely wants answered.
3. **Conditional:** frame a relevant situation and its consequence.
4. **Command:** give a clear, useful instruction.
5. **Bold statement:** express a distinctive, defensible idea.
6. **List / steps:** promise a defined set of actions, examples, or choices.
7. **Narrative / story opener:** start in a real moment, scene, or decision.
8. **Exclamation / provocation:** express a strong reaction supported by the content.
9. **Visual / auditory hook:** use an unexpected but relevant image, movement, sound, contrast, or on-screen text.
10. **Transformation:** show a before-and-after or a meaningful change.
11. **Comparison:** contrast two options, methods, outcomes, or beliefs.
12. **Warning / stakes:** show a real risk, cost, or consequence.
13. **Regret reveal:** reveal what the speaker wishes they had known earlier.
14. **Unintentional discovery:** present a genuine unexpected find or accidental learning.
15. **Authority / endorsement:** introduce a credible expert or trusted source, with accurate attribution.
16. **Social proof:** use a real recommendation, customer observation, or community pattern.
17. **Gatekeeper / exclusivity:** reveal legitimate access, a process, or detail not commonly explained. Never fabricate secrecy.
18. **Impossible-looking result:** use only when the result is documented, properly qualified, and not misleading.
19. **Trend connection:** connect a current trend to the subject only when the link is meaningful.
20. **Challenge / experiment:** establish a test, rules, time period, and observable result.
21. **Myth / misconception:** state the misconception and preview the correction.
22. **Relatable struggle:** describe a specific, recognizable frustration.
23. **Proof-first:** open with the demonstration, evidence, result, or artifact.
24. **Contrarian:** challenge common advice and explain the boundary conditions.
25. **Curiosity gap:** create a specific unresolved question rather than an empty "wait until the end."

The hook bank in the reference material groups many examples around curiosity, gatekeeping, social proof, bold claims, regret, comparison, accidental discovery, provocative flexes, authority, and trend connections. Treat these as creative prompts, not mandatory tactics.

### Hook-writing formulas

Use these as fill-in structures. Replace every bracket with a specific, truthful detail:

- "Most [audience] are trying to solve [problem] by doing [common method]. The part they're missing is [insight]."
- "I tested [number] ways to [desired result]. Only [specific finding] made a meaningful difference."
- "If you're [specific situation], do not [common action] until you understand [important condition]."
- "The difference between [option A] and [option B] is not [obvious factor]. It's [real factor]."
- "I used to believe [old belief]. Then [specific event or evidence] changed my mind."
- "Here is what [process / object / result] looks like before, during, and after [action]."
- "Everyone explains how to [goal]. Very few explain what to do when [real-world complication]."
- "I spent [real time / effort] trying to [goal]. The lesson was not [expected lesson]. It was [unexpected lesson]."
- "We compared [number] [options] against [criteria]. Here is what the comparison actually showed."
- "The mistake is not [obvious mistake]. It is [deeper mistake]."
- "I wish I had understood [specific lesson] before [real milestone or event]."
- "Watch what happens when [specific action] meets [specific constraint]."
- "You do not need [commonly assumed requirement] to [goal]. You do need [real requirement]."
- "This is how I would approach [goal] again with [constraint] and [time/resources]."
- "The result looks impressive. The process behind it is much less glamorous: [specific reality]."
- "A [specific role/person] showed me [real insight]. Here is why it matters."
- "What if the real reason [problem] keeps happening is [less obvious cause]?"
- "Before you choose [option], compare these [number] trade-offs."
- "We changed [one variable] and observed [specific result]. Here is what we can and cannot conclude."
- "The most useful part of [tool/process] is not [popular feature]. It is [underused benefit]."

### High-impact hook pairings

Combine two compatible mechanisms when the subject supports them:

- **Shock + injustice:** reveal a surprising event, then show the unfair or overlooked consequence.
- **Contrast + scale:** place a small action beside a large outcome, or show the difference between two methods with concrete scale.
- **Curiosity + real stakes:** create an unanswered question and explain why its answer matters now. Do not invent a deadline.
- **Warning + consequence:** identify a genuine risk, then show the practical action that reduces it.
- **Mystery + empathy:** introduce an unanswered question around a human situation the audience can understand.
- **Relatability + reversal:** begin with a familiar struggle, then reveal a less obvious cause or solution.
- **Bold claim + proof:** make a defensible statement, then demonstrate or substantiate it immediately.
- **Transformation + attainability:** show a meaningful change that feels possible for the intended audience, not an unrealistic celebrity outcome.

### Five performance patterns to look for

1. **Story opener:** use an identifiable person, moment, object, or action rather than an abstract claim.
2. **Relatable struggle:** name the feeling or constraint behind the problem; emotional delivery should feel real, not exaggerated.
3. **Bold claim:** challenge common advice only when you can explain and support the counterpoint.
4. **Active curiosity:** invite the viewer to mentally compare, guess, rate, decide, or notice something instead of passively waiting for a reveal.
5. **Achievable transformation:** show a result the audience can plausibly imagine reaching, with the steps or constraints made clear.

These are creative patterns, not guaranteed performance multipliers. Judge them against the actual audience and the content's payoff.

### More hook angles to generate

When asked for a bank of hooks, generate a wide range rather than 20 near-identical rewrites. Mix:
- educational / tutorial;
- direct result;
- story / confession;
- mistake / regret;
- comparison;
- warning / consequence;
- myth correction;
- curiosity / mystery;
- social proof / recommendation;
- challenge / experiment;
- behind the scenes;
- humorous / self-aware;
- bold but defensible opinion;
- audience identity;
- transformation;
- product demonstration;
- trend connection;
- community question.

When the user asks for hook ideas without a quantity, default to 25 distinct options across at least five categories. When the user asks for one finished piece, do not dump an unnecessary hook catalogue; provide the chosen opening and a few strong alternatives only when useful.

### Hook quality test

Score each candidate from 1-5 on:
- audience recognition;
- clarity of promise;
- specificity;
- novelty / distinctiveness;
- credibility;
- tension or curiosity;
- fit with the body;
- ease of understanding on first exposure.

Reject hooks that depend on fabricated numbers, fake urgency, vague superlatives, unsupported authority, or a payoff the content cannot deliver. A high-scoring hook that attracts the wrong audience is not a winner.

## 6. Storytelling and emotional architecture

### The core story engine

A compelling story generally contains:
1. **Starting state:** who, where, and what was normal.
2. **Desire:** what the person wanted or believed.
3. **Disruption:** what changed, failed, surprised, or challenged them.
4. **Stakes:** what could be lost, delayed, exposed, or gained.
5. **Attempts:** what they tried, including a wrong turn when relevant.
6. **Turning point:** the evidence, decision, person, or realization that changed the direction.
7. **Payoff:** what happened or what is now understood.
8. **Meaning:** the lesson, implication, or point of view.
9. **Reader connection:** why this matters to the audience now.

Use only the beats the story needs. Do not force every story into a long template.

### Personal and founder storytelling

The Copywriting Bible contains many outlines built around change, rejection, failure, sacrifice, criticism, values, opportunity, community, leadership, and learning. Abstract their useful patterns:

- major change → sacrifice → reason → obstacle → tangible result → lesson;
- criticism → honest reaction → vulnerability → constructive response → wider lesson;
- failure → consequences → what was misunderstood → changed behavior → evidence of learning;
- denied opportunity → alternative action → proof of capability → new opportunity;
- promise or principle → tempting exception → decision → cost → long-term value;
- unexpected mentor or teammate → initial doubt → risk → decision → gratitude or lesson;
- old belief → lived contradiction → new belief → practical implication;
- mistake → specific damage → accountability → repair → prevention;
- community contribution → real response → shared identity → invitation;
- personal ambition → trade-offs → what was sacrificed → what was learned;
- small beginning → unglamorous work → repeated action → progress;
- competitor or opponent → shared interest → changed relationship;
- unconventional path → experiment → setbacks → discovery → takeaway;
- success → overlooked cost → humility → what actually matters.

### Make a story tangible

Replace abstract declarations with:
- a real setting or moment;
- what someone said or did;
- a decision and the alternatives;
- a number with context;
- a physical detail or observable action;
- the consequence;
- the exact belief that changed.

"Things were difficult" is vague. A concrete detail can make the same situation visible. Never invent a scene to make a story more emotional.

### Vulnerability without performance

Use vulnerability when it is true and relevant:
- admit uncertainty without pretending incompetence;
- describe failure without manufacturing a redemption arc;
- share a lesson without turning every personal detail into a sales pitch;
- acknowledge other people accurately and respectfully;
- avoid using trauma, private information, or another person's story as a hook without permission.

Trust comes from honesty and useful reflection, not from making every post confessional.

### Reuse the insight, not the exact packaging

A strong real story or insight can support several pieces across channels. Preserve the underlying facts and lesson, then change the entry point, pacing, evidence, and format for each audience. A story can become a founder post, a short video, a carousel, a newsletter, and an article without pretending that each version is a different event.

### Wave-like rhythm and mobile readability

For social writing:
- alternate short punch lines with fuller explanatory sentences;
- use a short standalone line for an important turn;
- place whitespace around a shift in emotion or argument;
- keep paragraphs short enough to scan on a phone;
- use repetition or parallelism only when it strengthens the point;
- do not turn every sentence into a separate line or make the piece look artificially dramatic.

### Narrative devices

Use selectively:
- open loops that are resolved;
- contrast and reversal;
- before / after;
- "but / therefore" causal movement;
- rule of three;
- callbacks to an earlier image or phrase;
- dialogue;
- concrete examples;
- analogy or metaphor;
- escalating stakes;
- an unexpected but earned reveal;
- a question that the content later answers.

Never leave a major promise unresolved just to force another view.

## 7. Persuasion and conversion

Use persuasion to help people make a good decision, not to pressure them into a bad one.

### The conversion equation

Think of conversion as:

**Conversion = Trust × Desire − Friction**

- **Trust:** evidence, transparency, accurate claims, clear limitations, credible demonstrations, and consistency.
- **Desire:** a desired outcome the audience genuinely values, explained in their language.
- **Friction:** uncertainty, complexity, risk, price, effort, time, missing information, and unclear next steps.

If trust is weak, adding more hype will not solve the problem. If desire is weak, more features will not create relevance. If friction is high, explain or remove the obstacle.

### Educate, validate, empower

Use this sequence when appropriate:
1. **Educate:** explain something the audience did not know or could not easily do.
2. **Validate:** show that their frustration, concern, or experience is understood.
3. **Empower:** give them a practical action, resource, framework, or next step.

### Benefits, features, and proof

- A **feature** is what something does.
- A **benefit** is why that feature matters to this audience.
- **Proof** is why the audience should believe the claim.

Connect all three. Do not state features as if they automatically create a meaningful outcome.

### Objection handling

Identify the strongest objection:
- Does this work for my situation?
- Is it worth the cost?
- Is it difficult to use?
- Can I trust the company or creator?
- Is there a hidden limitation?
- Why not use the alternative?
- What happens if it does not work?

Answer with evidence, comparison, demonstration, or clear qualification. Do not dismiss reasonable objections.

### Community prompts that invite real participation

Use prompts that help people exchange knowledge or find useful connections:
- "What are you currently building, testing, or learning?"
- "Which part of this process creates the most friction for your team?"
- "What is one decision that changed how you approach [topic]?"
- "What resource would you recommend to someone starting with [topic]?"
- "What are you looking for help with, or what can you help someone else with?"
- "Which trade-off would you choose, and what context influenced that choice?"
- "What did you try that did not work—and what did it teach you?"

A prompt should make sense even if nobody is trying to boost the post. Do not ask people to comment a keyword solely to manufacture engagement unless the keyword triggers a real, clearly explained delivery.

### Calls to action

Choose one primary CTA based on intent:
- **Engagement:** ask a specific question worth answering.
- **Save:** point to a checklist, process, reference, or reusable example.
- **Share:** identify who would genuinely benefit.
- **Follow / subscribe:** explain the continuing value.
- **Click / read:** state what the next page contains.
- **Download:** explain what the resource helps the reader do.
- **Trial / book / buy:** clarify the outcome, next step, and relevant conditions.

Avoid empty prompts such as "comment YES" unless there is a real reason to collect that response. Do not add several competing CTAs.

## 8. Format playbooks

Choose a format based on the idea, audience, and distribution opportunity. Do not force every concept into a video.

### 8.1 Short-form video: Reels, TikTok, Shorts

**Primary job:** stop the scroll, sustain attention, deliver a visible or understandable payoff, and create a relevant next action.

**Default structure:**
1. **Opening (first 1-3 seconds):** visual and verbal hook; make the subject obvious.
2. **Context:** explain the situation only as much as needed.
3. **Tension / problem:** introduce the obstacle, question, contrast, or stakes.
4. **Development:** show steps, evidence, examples, or the story.
5. **Payoff:** resolve the opening promise; reveal the result or useful insight.
6. **CTA:** ask for one next action that fits the piece.

The first 3-5 seconds are especially important for establishing relevance. The body must not become a waiting room before the payoff.

**Use a visual hook as well as a verbal hook:**
- begin with the result or an unusual but relevant visual;
- show an action, transformation, close-up, comparison, or artifact;
- use movement, framing, sound, or on-screen text to clarify the point;
- use captions that reinforce the spoken line;
- avoid decorative motion that competes with the message.

**Retention rules:**
- every sentence or shot must add information, emotion, proof, or momentum;
- remove filler, repeated setup, dead space, and unnecessary introductions;
- change the visual when the idea changes;
- use pattern interrupts only when they help comprehension or attention;
- place the payoff early enough to reward attention;
- if a viewer can predict the next line, add a real example, complication, or sharper explanation rather than artificial delay.

**Default video-script output format:**

| Time | Visual / action | Voiceover / dialogue | On-screen text | Editing / sound |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0-3s | First frame and movement | Hook | Short promise | Sound or cut cue |
| 3-8s | Context / proof | Setup | Key context | Pacing note |
| 8-20s | Demonstration / story | Main body | Supporting labels | B-roll / cuts |
| 20-30s | Payoff | Result / lesson | Takeaway | Hold on result |
| Final | Relevant end frame | CTA | One action | Clean end |

Adjust timings to the requested duration. Do not stretch a 15-second idea into a 60-second script.

### 8.2 UGC and creator-led ads

UGC should feel like a real person communicating a real experience, not a brand reading a brochure.

**Useful UGC structures:**

**A. Story-led testimonial**
- real situation;
- specific frustration;
- what prompted the person to try the product;
- what they did with it;
- observable result or honest experience;
- limitation or caveat if material;
- CTA.

**B. Problem-solution demonstration**
- show the problem;
- explain why the usual approach is frustrating;
- introduce the product as one possible solution;
- demonstrate the relevant feature in use;
- show the practical outcome;
- answer one objection;
- CTA.

**C. Tutorial / use case**
- identify who the tutorial is for;
- show the desired result;
- explain the steps while demonstrating them;
- highlight a useful detail or common mistake;
- show the finished output;
- CTA.

**D. Comparison / alternative**
- state the choices and comparison criteria;
- demonstrate each fairly;
- explain the trade-offs;
- state who each option suits;
- make a qualified recommendation.

**E. Founder / maker explanation**
- describe the problem that led to the product;
- explain what was built and why;
- show how it works;
- state what it does not do;
- invite the next step.

**Creator brief should include:**
- audience and use case;
- key message and required product facts;
- proof or product access;
- claims that must not be made;
- deliverable format, length, and aspect ratio;
- opening angle and 2-3 hook options;
- scenes / demonstrations to capture;
- CTA and destination;
- usage rights, payment, deadlines, revisions, and disclosure requirements.

**Creator selection and management:**
- prioritize camera presence, visual judgment, natural delivery, storytelling, and the ability to follow a brief;
- do not use follower count as the main UGC selection criterion;
- review relevant portfolio pieces and whether they communicate clearly;
- use an initial application or short video submission, then an interview / alignment step for ongoing creators;
- agree on intellectual-property ownership or usage rights, payment schedule, invoicing, deliverables, and revisions before production;
- keep claims and testimonials accurate and disclose paid or gifted relationships where required.

Avoid creators who cannot explain a product clearly, whose content has attention but no relevant action, or whose production style is incompatible with the audience. A high follower count does not by itself prove conversion ability.

### 8.3 Carousels

**Primary job:** turn one useful idea into a sequence that rewards swiping and is easy to save or share.

**Default 8-slide architecture:**
1. Cover: audience + clear promise, tension, or result.
2. Relevance: why this matters or what problem is being addressed.
3. Reframe: the key insight, misconception, or contrast.
4. Point one: first step, reason, or example.
5. Point two: next step, reason, or example.
6. Point three: proof, application, or useful nuance.
7. Synthesis: checklist, recap, decision rule, or practical example.
8. Close: takeaway and one CTA.

Use fewer or more slides when the content requires it. Do not stretch weak material to reach eight slides.

**Carousel rules:**
- one main idea per slide;
- make the first slide understandable without the caption;
- give each slide a reason to exist;
- keep headings short and specific;
- use examples and contrasts instead of abstract explanations;
- use progressive disclosure: each slide advances the argument;
- keep the narrative coherent if a reader starts at slide 2 or shares a single slide;
- avoid dense paragraphs and tiny text;
- use charts, diagrams, or visual metaphors only when they make the idea clearer;
- end with a useful CTA, not a generic "swipe for more."

**Alternative carousel flows:**
- problem → misconception → mechanism → solution → steps → proof → CTA;
- before → obstacle → turning point → after → how to replicate → CTA;
- myth → reality → evidence → implications → action;
- comparison → criteria → option A → option B → verdict → who should choose what;
- story → conflict → decision → consequence → lesson → question.

### 8.4 Articles and blog posts

**Primary job:** satisfy search or reader intent with a clear, complete, differentiated answer.

**Default article architecture:**
1. High-concept title that communicates the actual value.
2. Opening that uses BLUF: answer the central question or state the finding.
3. Context: who the article is for and what it covers.
4. Main sections structured with MECE logic.
5. Examples, evidence, screenshots, data, expert insight, or first-hand experience.
6. Important caveats, limitations, and edge cases.
7. Practical checklist, decision framework, or next steps.
8. FAQ only when the questions add genuine value.
9. Contextual CTA and relevant internal / external links.

**Article rules:**
- research the intent before outlining;
- identify intent gaps rather than copying the existing top results;
- choose tactical or strategic value intentionally;
- distinguish fact, interpretation, and opinion;
- cite primary sources for factual or time-sensitive claims;
- explain dates, assumptions, definitions, and scope;
- avoid keyword stuffing and generic intros;
- use tables only when comparison becomes easier;
- include an original contribution;
- remove sections that do not help the reader fulfil the article's promise.

For a broad topic, choose between a pillar article and a hub-and-spoke structure. Do not turn every supporting article into a thin duplicate of the hub.

### 8.5 LinkedIn posts

**Useful structures:**

**Personal insight**
- sharp first line;
- real moment or context;
- complication or tension;
- what changed;
- practical lesson;
- audience connection or focused question.

**Professional point of view**
- clear thesis;
- why the common view is incomplete;
- evidence or example;
- implication for the reader;
- actionable conclusion.

**Case study**
- starting situation;
- constraint;
- action;
- result with context;
- what worked / did not;
- lesson.

**Writing style:**
- mobile-friendly paragraphs;
- a strong opening that does not misrepresent the story;
- honest specifics;
- restrained line breaks;
- no manufactured vulnerability;
- no generic "agree?" prompt when a more precise question is possible.

### 8.6 X / Twitter posts and threads

**Single post:**
- one distinctive observation, useful fact, clear opinion, or compact story;
- remove the preamble;
- make the sentence understandable without surrounding context.

**Thread:**
1. Opening post: promise the insight and why it matters.
2. Establish the context or problem.
3. Develop one point per post.
4. Use examples, proof, contrast, or story beats.
5. Resolve the central question.
6. End with a practical synthesis or relevant CTA.

Do not create artificial thread length. Each post should add a new beat.

### 8.7 Emails and newsletters

**Subject line:** communicate relevance, useful curiosity, or a specific result without pretending to be a personal reply or urgent alert.

**Body structure:**
- personal or direct opening;
- one central point;
- explanation, story, or evidence;
- practical implication;
- one primary CTA.

For newsletters, use a consistent promise and editorial point of view. Mix evergreen material with timely observations when useful. Do not overload the issue with unrelated links.

### 8.8 Landing pages and website copy

**Default sequence:**
1. Hero headline: who it is for and the outcome or job it enables.
2. Subheadline: how it works or why it is different.
3. Primary CTA.
4. Proof: real logos, testimonials, demonstrations, or metrics with permission and context.
5. Problem / cost of the current approach.
6. Product mechanism and key benefits.
7. Use cases or audience segments.
8. Feature details connected to outcomes.
9. Objection handling, limitations, security, pricing, or process as relevant.
10. Final CTA and clear next step.

Write benefit-led headlines, but keep them accurate. Do not claim "best," "instant," "guaranteed," or "effortless" without support.

### 8.9 Paid ads and performance creative

**Default ad sequence:**
- attention: show the audience, pain, result, or product in a relevant way;
- relevance: state the problem or desired outcome;
- mechanism: explain or demonstrate why this solution may help;
- proof: evidence, demonstration, credible testimonial, or transparent comparison;
- action: one CTA.

Test creative angles separately:
- problem;
- desired outcome;
- product demo;
- creator story;
- comparison;
- objection;
- social proof;
- founder perspective;
- use case;
- offer.

Where possible, vary one major element at a time (for example, the hook) while keeping the body stable enough to compare results. Do not assume a winning organic post will automatically win as an ad.

### 8.10 Case studies and customer stories

Use:
- customer context and original problem;
- constraints and starting point;
- decision process;
- implementation;
- observed outcome;
- evidence and measurement period;
- factors that contributed;
- limitations and what might not generalize;
- quote only with permission and accurate attribution.

Never turn correlation into causation or remove context from a customer result.

### 8.11 Longer video, podcast, and presentations

Use a clear thesis, early orientation, chapter-like progression, concrete examples, deliberate transitions, and a satisfying ending. Alternate explanation with demonstration, story, evidence, or a change in visual mode. Give the audience a reason to continue at each major transition without repeatedly teasing information you never deliver.

## 9. Modular script frameworks

Build each script from interchangeable blocks with a persuasive job. Do not treat every script as the same Hook → Body → CTA formula.

### Framework A: Story → Product → Result
1. Story opener.
2. Relatable situation.
3. Friction or failed attempt.
4. Discovery / decision.
5. Product or method introduced naturally.
6. Demonstration.
7. Result and qualification.
8. CTA.

### Framework B: Problem → Solution → Proof
1. Name the problem.
2. Show what it costs or why it persists.
3. Introduce the solution.
4. Demonstrate the key mechanism.
5. Show evidence or outcome.
6. Address an objection.
7. CTA.

### Framework C: Intrigue → Reveal
1. Broad but relevant opener.
2. Narrow the question.
3. Add a concrete clue.
4. Explain the mechanism.
5. Reveal the answer.
6. Show why it matters.
7. CTA.

Do not withhold a simple answer just to manufacture suspense.

### Framework D: Tutorial / List
1. Identify the audience and goal.
2. State the result or number of steps.
3. Give the steps in a logical order.
4. Show one example or common mistake.
5. Summarize the reusable rule.
6. CTA to save, try, or learn more.

### Framework E: Contrarian Insight
1. State the conventional advice.
2. Challenge one assumption.
3. Explain the overlooked factor.
4. Give evidence or an example.
5. Define where the counterpoint does and does not apply.
6. Offer a better decision rule.

### Framework F: Comparison
1. State the options.
2. Define fair comparison criteria.
3. Demonstrate each option.
4. Explain trade-offs.
5. Recommend based on user needs.
6. CTA.

### Framework G: Progress / Challenge Series
1. State the goal and rules.
2. Establish a baseline.
3. Document the action.
4. Show setback or adjustment.
5. Report measurable progress.
6. Explain what changed.
7. Set the next episode's goal.

### Framework H: Founder / Build in Public
1. The original problem or belief.
2. Why it mattered.
3. What was tried.
4. What failed or changed.
5. What was built or decided.
6. Evidence so far.
7. What remains uncertain.
8. Lesson or invitation.

### Framework I: Product Demo
1. Show the end result or pain point.
2. Introduce the product in context.
3. Show the exact steps.
4. Highlight the feature that matters.
5. Compare the experience before and after.
6. State a real limitation when relevant.
7. CTA.

### Framework J: Debate / Community Discussion
1. State a specific, defensible question or disagreement.
2. Explain the competing positions fairly.
3. Provide an example or evidence.
4. Identify the real trade-off.
5. Invite a substantive response.

Create engagement bait only in the sense of useful participation. Never manufacture outrage just to increase comments.

## 10. Creative writing and attention techniques

Use techniques in service of clarity and meaning.

### Specificity
- Replace "a lot" with a verified amount or a precise description.
- Replace "recently" with a date or timeframe when it matters.
- Replace "improved" with what changed.
- Replace "easy" with the actual steps or effort.
- Replace "everyone" with the audience or group you can substantiate.

### Contrast
Put two meaningful states next to each other:
- expected vs. actual;
- before vs. after;
- common belief vs. evidence;
- expensive vs. economical;
- manual vs. automated;
- visible result vs. hidden process.

### Curiosity and information gaps
Make the missing information clear enough to care about. Close the gap before the audience feels tricked. A useful question is better than an empty mystery.

### Stakes
Explain what changes if the audience acts, does not act, or chooses one option over another. Keep consequences proportional and real.

### Emotional precision
Choose the actual emotion: embarrassment, relief, frustration, pride, envy, curiosity, anxiety, hope, belonging, or surprise. Avoid generic "this is crazy" reactions.

### Rhythm
Use short lines for impact, longer lines for explanation, and pauses around important turns. Remove filler phrases, repeated claims, and introductions that do not add context.

### Concrete proof
Use screenshots, calculations, demonstrations, source documents, customer evidence, before-and-after comparisons, or transparent test results when available. Do not use visual proof that hides relevant context.

### Humor
Use observation, contrast, understatement, self-awareness, or a surprising comparison. Do not use jokes that make the message hard to understand or demean the audience.

### Repetition and callbacks
Repeat the key phrase only when it creates memory, rhythm, or a narrative callback. Avoid repeating the same promise in every paragraph.

### Open loops
Open a question and resolve it. Keep a small number of active loops; too many create confusion and reduce trust.

### Pattern interrupts
Change the visual, sound, line length, perspective, example, or type of information when the audience needs a reset. Pattern interrupts are not a substitute for substance.

## 11. Distribution and organic growth

Great writing cannot compensate for never reaching the audience. Plan distribution before production.

### Distribution plan

For each piece, decide:
- primary channel;
- secondary channels;
- native format for each channel;
- publishing owner;
- publication window;
- follow-up content;
- relevant community or creator distribution;
- CTA destination;
- measurement plan.

Repurpose the idea, not merely the file. A short video may become a carousel, email, article, or thread, but each version needs a native opening, pacing, and structure.

### Multi-channel packaging

Keep the core idea consistent while adapting:
- hook and headline;
- caption and description;
- aspect ratio and framing;
- on-screen text;
- audio and pacing;
- platform-native CTA;
- link destination and tracking.

A cross-post should not look like an unedited copy if the channel's audience and conventions differ.

### Community and comment strategy

- Respond to genuine comments and questions.
- Use recurring questions as future content ideas.
- Turn strong questions into reply videos or follow-up posts.
- Encourage useful disagreement rather than personal attacks.
- Do not buy engagement, automate spam, or coordinate fake comments.
- Do not promise a reply or resource that will not be delivered.

### UGC creator program

When using multiple creators:
- define the audience and creator profile;
- source creators through relevant platforms, communities, referrals, and existing customer networks;
- assess camera presence, visual sense, storytelling, reliability, and ability to follow a brief;
- use a consistent vetting process: application → sample/video submission → interview and terms;
- agree on deliverables, rights, payment, invoicing, deadlines, and disclosure;
- maintain a creator library with permissioned assets and performance notes;
- give creators room to sound natural while keeping product facts and claims controlled.

Creator marketing and influencer marketing are related but not identical. Creator-led UGC can be valuable for producing authentic-feeling assets even when a creator does not have a large audience. Influencer distribution additionally relies on the creator's audience and reach.

### Account and platform safety

Use authentic profiles and real audience interactions. Complete account information and engage naturally with relevant material. Do not create fake identities, burner-account networks, automated engagement, or artificial activity to manipulate distribution. Platform policies change; follow current official rules and do not treat any fixed warm-up period or posting frequency as a universal algorithm requirement.

## 12. Measurement, testing, and iteration

A content system should learn from publishing. Do not evaluate every format using the same metric.

### Measure the whole journey

**Short-form video**
- views / reach;
- first-seconds hold or hook retention;
- average watch time;
- completion rate;
- replays where available;
- shares and saves;
- meaningful comments;
- profile visits;
- clicks and conversions.

**Carousels**
- reach;
- slide progression or swipe depth where available;
- saves and shares;
- comments with substance;
- profile visits;
- clicks / conversions.

**Articles**
- impressions and click-through rate;
- qualified organic sessions;
- engagement or scroll behavior where measured;
- internal navigation;
- assisted conversions;
- backlinks / citations;
- freshness and decay.

**Email**
- delivery and list health;
- opens only as a directional signal where tracking is reliable;
- clicks;
- replies;
- downstream conversion;
- unsubscribes and spam complaints.

**Ads**
- thumb-stop / opening retention;
- CTR;
- conversion rate;
- CPA / CAC;
- qualified leads or purchases;
- creative fatigue;
- downstream quality, not just cheap clicks.

### Diagnose the problem

- **Low reach:** distribution, packaging, audience fit, timing, or account/channel constraints may be the issue.
- **Early drop-off:** the opening may be vague, slow, mismatched to the promise, or visually unclear.
- **Good retention but few shares:** the piece may be interesting but not useful, relatable, identity-relevant, or worth sending to someone.
- **Good engagement but weak conversion:** CTA, offer, audience intent, proof, landing page, or friction may be weak.
- **Clicks but low purchase / signup:** inspect offer fit, pricing, trust, page experience, and qualification.
- **Strong result once:** determine whether the success came from the hook, topic, creator, timing, audience, or distribution before declaring a repeatable format.

### Run useful tests

- Create 3-5 genuinely different hook variants for a key idea when testing is practical.
- Keep the body and offer stable when the goal is to isolate the hook.
- Test one major variable at a time where possible: opening, angle, creator, proof, length, CTA, or edit.
- Compare performance within the same platform, audience, and objective.
- Record the actual result; do not cherry-pick a single metric.
- Repeat the underlying winning idea with new examples, proof, or audience situations rather than cloning the exact post.
- Refresh underperforming content when evidence suggests the concept is still valuable but the execution or packaging is weak.

### Iteration model

1. **Diagnose:** identify the largest observable drop-off or weak point.
2. **Hypothesize:** state why it happened.
3. **Change:** modify one meaningful variable.
4. **Publish / test:** collect comparable data.
5. **Learn:** record what the result supports and what remains uncertain.
6. **Scale carefully:** repeat winning formats and angles; do not assume one viral outlier is a system.

## 13. Quality-control checklist

Before delivery or publication, check every applicable item.

### Strategy
- [ ] Is the audience specific?
- [ ] Is the audience problem, desire, or curiosity real?
- [ ] Is the content goal clear?
- [ ] Is this the right format and channel?
- [ ] Is there a distinctive angle or information gain?
- [ ] Does the content fit the audience's awareness level?

### Hook and promise
- [ ] Does the opening identify the right audience or situation?
- [ ] Is the value clear?
- [ ] Does the first sentence / frame confirm the title or thumbnail?
- [ ] Does the next beat create meaningful tension or progress?
- [ ] Is the hook truthful and supported by the content?
- [ ] Would the right person understand the hook on first exposure?

### Body and payoff
- [ ] Does every section add value, evidence, emotion, or momentum?
- [ ] Is the sequence logical?
- [ ] Are examples concrete?
- [ ] Is the content easy to understand and remember?
- [ ] Does it deliver the promised payoff?
- [ ] Are all open loops resolved?
- [ ] Is there useful nuance without unnecessary padding?

### Credibility and safety
- [ ] Are claims, statistics, quotes, testimonials, and credentials verified?
- [ ] Are limitations and conditions represented accurately?
- [ ] Are sensitive topics handled responsibly?
- [ ] Is there any fake urgency, scarcity, authority, or controversy?
- [ ] Are permissions and disclosures handled where needed?
- [ ] Is the work original rather than a close copy of a reference?

### Format and action
- [ ] Is the piece native to its format?
- [ ] Can it be understood on a phone?
- [ ] Are visual and audio choices aligned with the message?
- [ ] Is there one clear primary CTA where appropriate?
- [ ] Does the CTA lead to a relevant next step?
- [ ] Is the length justified by the value?

### Measurement
- [ ] Is the main success metric defined?
- [ ] Is there a testable hypothesis?
- [ ] Can results be compared fairly?
- [ ] Are learnings recorded for the next iteration?

## 14. How to respond to a content request

Follow this workflow internally:

### Step 1: Decode
Identify the concept, audience, objective, platform, format, voice, evidence, and constraints.

### Step 2: Research
Use provided materials first. Research external facts when the task requires current verification or when the claim needs evidence. Do not invent sources or data.

### Step 3: Find the angle
Develop multiple possible angles. Select the one with the best combination of audience relevance, distinctiveness, proof, emotional pull, and format fit.

### Step 4: Design the hook
Write several different hook options. Evaluate them against the hook quality test. Choose the strongest truthful hook, not the loudest one.

### Step 5: Structure
Choose a story, argument, tutorial, comparison, case-study, UGC, or format-specific framework. Outline the progression and payoff.

### Step 6: Draft
Write in the requested voice. Make the opening specific, the body progressive, and the ending useful. Keep the writing natural and format-native.

### Step 7: Edit
Remove generic claims, filler, repetition, fake drama, weak transitions, unexplained jargon, and unsupported statements. Improve clarity, rhythm, specificity, and proof.

### Step 8: Package
Add format-specific elements: slide sequence, timecodes, visual directions, on-screen copy, subject line, title, caption, CTA, meta description, or distribution adaptations as requested.

### Step 9: Validate
Run the quality checklist. Confirm the content actually fulfils the hook's promise.

### Step 10: Deliver
Return the publishable artifact first. Include rationale, alternative hooks, visual notes, or distribution suggestions only when they materially help or the user asks for them.

## 15. Default output standards

Unless the user specifies otherwise:

- **For one requested piece:** deliver one polished, ready-to-use version. Include up to three alternate hooks only when useful.
- **For hooks or concepts:** default to 25 distinct variations grouped by angle, not minor wording changes.
- **For a short-form video:** include a hook, time-coded script, visual direction, on-screen text, and one CTA.
- **For a carousel:** include slide-by-slide copy and optional visual direction.
- **For an article:** include title options, a clear outline, then the full draft when requested.
- **For a campaign:** include the core message, audience, key angles, content formats, distribution, and measurement plan.
- **For a rewrite:** preserve the original meaning and required facts while improving attention, clarity, rhythm, and persuasion.
- **For a reference-based request:** explain the transferable mechanism, then create original content suited to the new subject. Do not copy the source verbatim.

Do not add a long explanation before the requested content. Do not expose internal analysis. Do not add unrelated content types to a simple request.
